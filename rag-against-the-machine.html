RAG / Evaluation

RAG Against the Machine

Building a RAG got cheaper. Evaluating its accuracy and efficiency remains challenging.

(Written 04/11/2026. 5 min read)

AI-assisted coding makes it increasingly easy to build something that looks like a working product. Two questions remain harder: is it useful, and how do we know how well it works?

I’m leaving the business case aside. Here, I’ll focus on answer quality, efficiency and the engineering trade-offs behind them.

During the RAG hype, I was a Machine Learning Engineer working on more classical problems: supervised learning on tabular data, pipelines, A/B testing. Then 2026 arrived, and RAG expertise seemed to have become part of the job description. My side projects had mostly explored music, so I had some catching up to do.

I’m still learning. What I can share is a three-day side project answering questions about documents from the Boletín Oficial del Estado, Spain’s official state gazette. The app runs on Azure. You can try it here.

Building from scratch can be a good way to learn and an inefficient way to deliver. This time, I started from boe-rag-assistant, an open-source project by Gonzalo López Crespo, then used Claude Code to help me investigate, deploy and iterate on it.

1. Wake Up

As a rule of thumb, I expect more computation and better frontier models to solve many of today’s technical puzzles (Sutton’s Bitter Lesson), whether in six months, two years or longer. So part of engineering is knowing where craft beats force (más vale maña que fuerza) and where the latest established model or solution is the no-brainer.

For instance, a small collection may not need retrieval at all. My blog fits in a prompt: about 18,000 tokens, or $0.002 per question with GPT-6 Luna and $0.04 with GPT-6 Sol at list prices. My BOE sample does not: about 16 million tokens, roughly 15 times their context window.

Even so, in an earlier local experiment I built retrieval and an MCP server for that same blog, probably without needing either. A prompt with the whole blog could ask for cited answers. MCP earns its place when the data doesn’t fit in the prompt or several clients share the same tools.

In the BOE project, coverage is one more thing to measure, and my retrieval benchmark can’t see it, because its questions only cover documents already in the corpus. The app holds a sample for cost reasons: 26 of the 207 Section I documents I checked across 50 sampled days.

Scale matters too. A retrieval score only holds for the collection it was measured on. Doubling my corpus raised memory and slightly lowered ranking quality. I estimate the full BOE would be about ten times larger, and I haven’t tested that.

But solving a puzzle is different from resolving a trade-off. Accuracy, latency, cost and coverage are tied together: widening coverage takes more memory and can lower accuracy, while sending more context costs more and takes longer. Better models may change what is possible and at what cost, but we still have to decide which balance serves the user. To decide well, we need to measure what matters. And evaluating can be the hardest part.

2. Bulls on Parade

Once the pipeline worked, I compared retrieval approaches.

Dense embeddings, hybrid retrieval, reciprocal rank fusion, a cross-encoder reranker. Each component had a plausible justification. Each needed to demonstrate a benefit.

The production hybrid combined BM25 and dense retrieval. On the 20-question gold set, its Recall@5 was 95%, compared with 85% for either method alone.

But the result changed with the questions.

For 14 questions asking what a particular law or regulation covered, BM25 found the labeled supporting passage in the top five every time. The served hybrid did so for 9 questions.

The cross-encoder was not a consistent improvement either. On an earlier corpus, it improved ranking on the small gold set but worsened results on the silver and norm-reference sets, while adding latency. Because those experiments used a different corpus, their scores cannot simply be compared with the current production figures.

More machinery had not earned an automatic place in production.

This is not evidence that BM25 always beats embeddings or that reranking is unnecessary. It is evidence that the answer depends on the corpus, questions, model and constraints.

Más vale maña que fuerza.

Efficiency also extended beyond inference cost. An inexpensive model call did not compensate for a slow cold start. Keeping an instance warm could improve responsiveness but increase the bill. Expanding coverage increased memory requirements and made retrieval more demanding.

3. Testify

Evaluation produced the most interesting questions.

What does “correct” mean? Correct according to the retrieved fragments? According to a reference answer? Complete enough to address the question? Supported by the particular source being cited?

I found it useful to separate three checks:

  • Retrieval: did the system find the labeled supporting evidence?
  • Groundedness: does the retrieved evidence support the generated answer?
  • Correctness: does the answer satisfy the expected facts and requirements?

These checks overlap, but none replaces the others. An answer can be grounded in the retrieved evidence while addressing the wrong question, repeat an error in a source, or provide the right answer without supporting retrieved evidence.

Abstention belongs in this picture too. If the available evidence cannot answer the question, acknowledging that limitation can be the correct response. Refusing an answerable question is a different failure.

A correctness evaluation can cover both cases, provided the references and rubric specify the expected behavior. I would still report them separately to distinguish excessive refusal from unsupported answering.

My existing evaluation also taught me to read percentages carefully. Its 90.5% “corrección” was an average of judge-assigned ratings, including partial credit. It did not mean that exactly 90.5% of answers passed a binary test. Likewise, 98% fidelity was not a count of individually verified claims.

An LLM judge made scoring easier to automate. It did not make the scoring objective. References still needed validation, partial-credit ratings needed a clearer rubric, and judge behavior needed checking.

There was another limitation: the project’s existing answer-quality evaluation dated from July, before my retrieval changes. Reproducible historical measurements were useful, but they were not measurements of today’s complete system.

The next dimension I want to investigate is concision.

Lo bueno, si breve, dos veces bueno.

Short answers reduce reading effort and leave fewer opportunities to introduce unsupported claims. But omitting an exception can make a legal answer misleading.

The goal is the shortest answer that remains correct and complete, preserving the relevant conditions, exceptions and supporting citations.

Concision is hard. I probably failed to produce a Minimum Viable Post here.
